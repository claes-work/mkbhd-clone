# I Visited Apple's Secret iPhone Testing Labs! (@mkbhd, 2024-06-07)

## Summary
A behind-the-scenes look at **Apple's iPhone durability testing labs** (a follow-up to a viral Twitter thread). Three
kinds of testing: **water resistance** — he explains the **IP rating code** (Ingress Protection: first digit = solids,
second = liquids; iPhone went from not-rated → IP67 on the iPhone 7 → IP68 now), demonstrated via a drip ceiling
(IPX4), rotating jets (IPX5), a literal fire hose (IPX6), and sealed **pressurized tanks** simulating depth (IPX7/8);
**drop testing** — an **Epson industrial robot** that repeats the exact same drop on different surfaces (granite,
marble, asphalt, wood) under **ultra-bright lights and high-speed Phantom cameras** (you can see the iPhone 15 Pro's
titanium *wobble* on impact); and **shaking tests** — trays of phones vibrated thousands of times at programmed
frequencies (a motorcycle, a car, a subway) to simulate years of wear. The framing idea he draws out: **durability and
repairability sit on a sliding scale** — the more durable (sealed, glued, waterproof) you make a phone, the harder it
is to repair. He puts this to **John Ternus (Apple's head of hardware engineering)**, who argues a product that never
fails is objectively better for the customer *and* the planet (fewer repairs = fewer new materials), that the bookends
aren't always mutually exclusive, and that batteries still need a repair path — and reveals Apple breaks **~10,000
prototype iPhones** in durability testing before shipping one. Marques's closing caveat: **"if the goal is a phone that
never breaks, they probably shouldn't make it from glass — glass is glass, glass breaks"** (crediting
JerryRigEverything).

## Key claims (paraphrase, dated)
- [2024-06-07] **The IP rating, demystified.** "'IP is Ingress Protection — first number for solids, second for
  liquids. iPhones weren't rated until the iPhone 7 (IP67); now everything's IP68, meaning it can be underwater for an
  extended period. Earbuds are often IPX4 — no dust rating, but fine for sweat and splashes.'"
- [2024-06-07] **Durability testing is elaborate.** "'They torture phones with water (a drip ceiling for IPX4, rotating
  jets for IPX5, a literal fire hose for IPX6, pressurized sealed tanks for IPX7/8). A programmed Epson robot repeats
  the exact same drop onto granite, marble, asphalt, wood while Phantom high-speed cameras capture it — you can see the
  iPhone 15 Pro's titanium wobble on impact. And rooms of shaking machines simulate years of a motorcycle/car/subway
  vibration.'"
- [2024-06-07] **Durability vs. repairability is a sliding scale.** "'The more I thought about it, durability feels
  directly at odds with repairability — a sliding scale. The more durable you make the thing (seals, adhesives,
  waterproofing), the less repairable it is. All the design decisions push it back and forth along that scale.'"
- [2024-06-07] **~10,000 prototypes broken before one ships (per Ternus).** "'John Ternus told me they go through
  sometimes 10,000 prototype iPhones in durability testing before they ship a final phone. It seems insane they'll make
  10,000 phones to break before shipping one.' (Attributed to Ternus.)"
- [2024-06-07] **Glass is glass.** "'In a dream world, if the goal is the ultimate phone that never breaks, they
  probably shouldn't make it from glass — because in the words of JerryRigEverything, glass is glass, glass breaks. But
  they're pushing as far toward that never-fails end as possible.'"

## Notable verbatim quotes
> "It actually feels like it's basically a sliding scale, with durability on one side and repairability on the other."
> "The most fascinating thing is they're trying to simulate years of wear and tear in the real world… in a small room
> under the basement of Apple Park."
> "Glass is glass. Glass breaks." (crediting JerryRigEverything)
