# The Problem with this Humanoid Robot ★★

## Summary

An "old-school straight-to-camera rant" about the **1X NEO** humanoid home-chore robot — and,
more broadly, about **the AI promise gap**. The NEO's marketing sells a Rosie-the-Robot dream
(folds laundry, does dishes, waters plants, all autonomously), but Joanna Stern's reporting
revealed **100% of the demoed tasks were teleoperated by a human in a VR headset**. Marques's
core objection isn't the price — it's **the gap between what a product can do today and what
it promises as it takes your money today**. He uses Tesla self-driving as the parallel:
early adopters become the beta-testers/training-data engine to close that gap. A landmark
statement of his "sell the product, not the dream" doctrine. ★★.

## Key claims

- **[2025-10-30]** NEO (1X) is pitched as a 5'6", 66lb, self-charging humanoid that autonomously
  does every chore you don't want to — "this feels like it could be the best tech announcement of
  all time... **except this isn't real. And I mean that in the most literal way possible.**"
  (quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** It's a **pre-order** ($500/mo subscription or $20,000 to own; $200 refundable
  deposit; US deliveries "next year... allegedly"). **The price isn't his problem** — a first-gen
  bleeding-edge robot was never going to be cheap, and the target is "people whose time is worth
  more than the money." (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** **His problem is the gap.** People watching the marketing assume built-in AI
  that recognizes objects, navigates, and learns the house. But per **Joanna Stern's** hands-on,
  **100% of the tasks 1X showed her were remotely teleoperated by a human in a VR headset with
  controllers.** In the ~10-min keynote, only **two scenes** are labeled autonomous (open the
  door; put away a non-glass cup). "Everything else... we can safely assume is remote controlled."
  (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** **"There seems to be a bit of a lost art in waiting for a tech product to be
  actually finished before announcing and unveiling it."** He recalls when a company would demo a
  product and the next slide said "on sale tomorrow" — "that hype would carry right into the buying
  cycle. That was sick." Now, "you end up selling the dream before you sell the actual product.
  And that is dangerous." (quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** Same failure mode as the **Humane Pin, Rabbit r1, and even Apple Intelligence**
  — "big promises, but the capabilities are still over here while the promise is all the way over
  here." (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** **Tesla self-driving is the parallel**: to make the robot real, 1X needs a smart
  AI that learns the home, so — like Tesla using millions of cars and "people essentially beta
  testing for Tesla" to gather training data — they go **right back to early adopters**. NEO's site
  even has an **"expert mode"**: when the robot can't do a task, you schedule a **teleoperating
  employee to look through its sensors into your home** and do it so the robot can learn. (paraphrase)
- **[2025-10-30]** The privacy/beta-tester bargain: buyers "are basically going to be a beta tester
  for some unknown number of years" and "give up some pretty serious privacy" (remote-viewable
  cameras/mics in the home; the CEO told Joanna they'd "probably blur faces and maybe let you
  geofence" rooms). "Is there enough of those people? I don't know." (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** The deeper point: **"this is the new AI promise problem we have... all these AI
  products are promising the world way, way before they have a product that's ready."** He stresses
  he *wants* AI to do useful productive things — "it's just the gap. The gap, that's the issue. And
  closing that gap is so, so hard." The cruel irony: those who'd benefit most (the elderly, people
  with disabilities) are the least likely to be early adopters. (quote)
- **[2025-10-30]** Discloses 1X **emailed him from a random Gmail in January 2025 offering a
  sponsored video; he didn't respond** ("I didn't think it was anywhere close to real"). Ends with
  characteristic humility: "we might look back at this in 12 months and I look like an idiot... but
  history seems to be suggesting I'll probably be right about this one, at least for now."
  (paraphrase + quote)
