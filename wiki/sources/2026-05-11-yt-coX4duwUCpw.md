# Why New Smartphone Cameras Feel Worse ★★

## Summary

Marques takes the same photo with **every iPhone from 1 to 17** (and every Galaxy S, every
Pixel) and finds the surprising result: **the latest generation isn't always clearly the best**
for regular daylight photos. His explanation is a camera-philosophy landmark: since any phone
from the last ~5 years takes a fine daylight photo, makers now optimize for the **hard edge
cases** (low light, zoom, backlit) — but the aggressive computational processing that saves a
bad shot, when applied to a **normal photo that doesn't need it, makes it look worse** (flat,
haloed, over-lit). The over-processed look is real, and **"some tuning is definitely still
needed."**

## Key claims

- **[2026-05-11]** The experiment: the **same photo on every iPhone 1→17** (and every Galaxy S,
  every Pixel; daylight, low-light, backlit). Surprising result — **"it doesn't always look like the
  latest generation is far and away the best."** (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2026-05-11]** Why: cameras improved dramatically until **phones stopped getting bigger (~2010s)**
  — "we're maxing out how much phone you can carry; it's physics." So **any phone from the last ~5
  years takes a perfectly usable daylight photo** — the iPhone 17 and iPhone 11 shots "almost look
  like the same phone" (the 17's bigger sensor just gives more background blur). Good daylight "doesn't
  separate them anymore." (paraphrase)
- **[2026-05-11]** So the real separation is now in **edge cases** — low light, fast subjects, deep
  zoom, **backlit** scenes. A backlit Pixel 10 shot uses "a ton of computational photography — multi-
  frame HDR, tone mapping, exposure, face detection" to show his face, hoodie, AND the blue sky at
  once (vs the pre-HDR Nexus 4 showing "how terrible the scene is supposed to look"). It's **the self-
  correcting basketball hoop again — "no matter what you point it at, you get a good picture; it
  refuses to spit out a bad one."** (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2026-05-11]** **The twist — the pursuit of the never-miss camera has made regular daylight photos
  slightly worse.** The **more aggressively a phone throws every trick to save a bad shot, the more it
  throws those same tricks at a normal photo that doesn't need it,** messing it up. The every-Galaxy-S
  sequence shows it: HDR arrives at the **S9** (recovers the blown-out window), but by the **S26** "I
  don't like this photo anymore" — haloing around the window/plant, unnatural glow, a face lit brighter
  than it actually is. The **S23** (and the **iPhone 11 vs 17**) looks **more natural** — "a little less
  HDR, but less haloing… it just overall looks better," and the Shorts comments agree. (paraphrase + quote)
- **[2026-05-11]** The fix is **tuning discipline**: makers know excess tone-mapping/HDR/face-detection
  "can look kind of weird on a regular photo," so **"it's all about knowing when to turn it up and when
  to turn it down"** — up to save a bad situation, down when it's not needed. (quote)
- **[2026-05-11]** The tell you can always see: the **gap between the viewfinder and the processed
  photo** — "you can always see it snap into place," and **the harder the scene, the more dramatic that
  snap.** "If the iPhone 11 has more natural color than the 17, and the S23 looks better than the S26,
  then some more tuning is definitely still needed." (Apps can turn post-processing down/off.) (quote)
