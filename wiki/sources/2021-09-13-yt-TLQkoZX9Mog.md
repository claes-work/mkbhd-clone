# Real Thoughts on Tech Leaks! (@mkbhd, 2021-09-13)

## Summary
A landmark media-literacy commentary on leak culture, opening with the 2010 Gizmodo stolen-iPhone-4 saga. The
durable framework: **two types of leaks** — the **serious kind** (industry secrets / competitive stakes, like the
iPhone-4 teardown showing unreleased parts to competitors) and the **casual, aesthetic, entertainment kind**
(mostly "how will it look"). His stance: pay attention to the first, largely ignore the second. Plus: **companies
intentionally leak** (OnePlus's slow-drip pre-launch strategy), **leaks take some air out of launches**, and
**"it's basically impossible to generate the correct level of hype — it's all just a show."**

## Key claims (paraphrase, dated)
- [2021-09-13] **The Gizmodo iPhone-4 leak — the biggest of all time.** "'2010: an Apple engineer left a disguised
  unreleased iPhone 4 at a bar; someone sold it to Gizmodo, who published a teardown. **What made it such a big
  leak wasn't the aesthetics early — it was the industry secret: they showed unreleased parts to the entire world,
  including Apple's competitors. That's the type of leak that hurts companies like Apple the most.**' (Police
  raided the editor's home.)'"
- [2021-09-13] **THE TWO TYPES OF LEAKS.** "'**(1) The serious type — industry secrets, competitive advantage at
  stake (supply-chain leaks, ransom-of-blueprints). (2) The casual type — not super important, mostly aesthetic,
  for entertainment.** I pay attention to the first; the second is usually not worth much energy.' A John Prosser
  leak of the exact announcement date, confirmed hours later? **"Shout out to John, great channel — but I don't
  care. That's purely for entertainment, with almost no effect on people who aren't following it hardcore."**'"
- [2021-09-13] **Companies intentionally leak — the OnePlus slow-drip.** "'Companies **intentionally leak little
  things** — if you saw all that hype being generated, you'd want to assert control over the narrative. **OnePlus's
  pre-launch strategy is to drip a nugget every few days (the chip, the charging speed, the RAM) — annoying, but
  smart: the slow drip acts like a leak, generating hype.** What people really want at the announcement is simple:
  the design, the features, and the price.'"
- [2021-09-13] **Why it's all aesthetic — and the iPhone 14 example.** "'The most entertaining leaks are **completely
  aesthetic — "how will it look" is one of the most important things people wonder** (price is last to finalize
  and hardest to leak). The iPhone 14 trended on Twitter (a Prosser video) **before we'd even seen the iPhone 13 —
  a year out, 100% aesthetics, no real feature speculation, a whole year of hype before anything can be proven
  wrong.**'"
- [2021-09-13] **DO LEAKS RUIN LAUNCHES? — a bit cynical.** "'**Following leaks takes some air out of the surprise
  — but those announcements are just more hype anyway.** For people who DON'T follow (a "sweet level of innocence
  I kind of miss"), the event is genuinely new. But the announcement isn't a spec rollout — **it's a high-budget,
  highly-refined show designed to make you want to buy.** If you DO follow every leak, **the announcement is a
  guaranteed letdown — either it's not what the leaks suggested (disappointing) or it's exactly what they
  suggested (no surprises).**'" A promotable read on hype.
- [2021-09-13] **"It's basically impossible to generate the correct level of hype."** "'It's **basically
  impossible to generate the correct level of hype — it's probably going to be overhyped no matter what. It's all
  a big show, it's fun, it's entertaining.** Leak culture is here to stay — a new dimension of watching companies
  duke it out, picking teams — and **as long as we know it's just entertainment that feeds the consumerism it's
  part of, have your fun.**'"
