# Is the iPhone 'Illegal'? (@mkbhd, 2024-04-07)

## Summary
An explainer on the **US Department of Justice's antitrust suit against Apple** (the iPhone as an illegal US monopoly),
built on his recurring frame: **"every big public company has two reasons for anything they say — the reason for the
public, and the real reason."** The scale that matters: the iPhone is **~60% US market share (near 90% with young
people) but only ~25% worldwide** — trending toward monopoly *in the US specifically*. He lives inside the **walled-
garden analogy**: it's not that any single integration is illegal, it's the **walls** that make it hard to leave. Two
examples from the DOJ's 88-page filing: the **Apple Watch works great with iPhone and only with iPhone** (a Garmin
gets none of the camera-shutter, Fitness, quick-reply, notification integrations — "all or nothing"; same with AirPods
/ AirTags), so switching phones means re-buying your whole ecosystem; and **iMessage** — the DOJ literally cites Tim
Cook on stage (**"buy your mom an iPhone"**), and internal emails show Apple knew putting iMessage on Android would
make it easier to leave, so **iMessage is "one of the biggest, thickest walls."** His verdict: **Apple is "technically
guilty of all these things"** — but they're not the only ones doing it (the Pixel Watch doesn't work with iPhone
either); they just happen to hold the **pseudo-monopoly**. The durable insight is the **China contrast**: because
**WeChat is a super-app everyone has**, phone makers there compete ruthlessly on hardware merit — **"lots of gardens,
very few walls,"** which is what healthy competition looks like. The suit is mainly a way to **lower the walls** so
companies compete on innovation again. (RCS is coming to iPhone in 2024 but "will probably still be green bubbles.")

## Key claims (paraphrase, dated)
- [2024-04-07] **The public reason and the real reason.** "'Every big public company has two reasons for any
  public-facing statement: the reason for the public, and the real reason. That's the lens for this whole thing.'"
- [2024-04-07] **The iPhone is a US-specific near-monopoly.** "'US iPhone share is ~60%, near 90% with young people;
  worldwide it's ~25%. So it's specifically trending toward a monopoly in the United States, which is who's suing.'"
- [2024-04-07] **Vertical integration vs. the walls.** "'If I build a smartwatch that only works with my smartphone,
  careful — that might be illegal. The Apple Watch works perfectly with the iPhone and only with the iPhone; a Garmin
  gets none of those integrations — it's all or nothing. Same with AirPods and AirTags. The individual products
  working well together isn't the problem — it's the walls that make it really hard to leave once you're in.'"
- [2024-04-07] **iMessage is the biggest, thickest wall.** "'The DOJ cites Tim Cook on stage: "buy your mom an iPhone."
  Internal emails have Apple execs admitting iMessage on Android would make it easier to switch away. Ask any young
  person in the US why they use an iPhone and a lot of them say something to do with iMessage. It's one of the biggest,
  thickest walls of the garden.'"
- [2024-04-07] **Apple is technically guilty — but they're not alone; they're just dominant.** "'Is Apple making other
  products worse, or making their own really good and not letting outsiders in? Honestly it's kind of both. They're
  technically guilty of all of it — but the Pixel Watch doesn't work with iPhone either. Apple just happens to be in
  the pseudo-monopoly position right now.'"
- [2024-04-07] **China / WeChat — lots of gardens, few walls.** "'In China, WeChat is a super-app everyone has —
  messaging, payments, taxis, bills, food. Because everyone has WeChat, Huawei/Xiaomi/Oppo/iPhone compete ruthlessly on
  hardware merit. That's lots of gardens and very few walls — a good competitive landscape. This lawsuit is mainly a
  way to lower the walls so we compete on innovation again. (What *would* be illegal: if Tencent made a phone and gave
  it exclusive WeChat access — then, monopoly.)'"

## Notable verbatim quotes
