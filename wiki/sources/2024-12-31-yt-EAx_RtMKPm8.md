# The Honey Scam: Explained (@mkbhd, 2024-12-31)

## Summary
An investigative breakdown of the **Honey scandal** — "the greatest scam in the history of the creator economy" —
surfaced by creator **MegaLag** (with Marques in the thumbnail). Honey **claims** to be a free browser extension that
finds you the best coupon codes at checkout (17M Chrome users, one of the biggest-ever YouTube sponsors, easy to work
with). But two shady things: **(1) affiliate-code poaching** — because Honey is the last step before checkout, hitting
"Apply Honey" **silently replaces the creator's affiliate code with Honey's own, every time**, so Honey collects the
referral revenue no matter who actually sent you (stealing from creators and journalistic outlets). **(2) the double-
speak that makes it a scam** — Honey tells *users* "use us to get the best coupons" while telling *retailers* "partner
with us and you choose which (worse) codes show up, so you prevent customers from getting the best deals" for a 3–5%
commission. **"They can't both be true — you have to be lying to one side, and they're lying to the users."** As
Austin Evans put it, **"they pay creators up front for the ad and then steal from their back pockets."** The kicker:
**Honey is owned by PayPal — this isn't a side hustle, it's the business model.** Marques's personal accountability: he
had **three Honey-sponsored videos** (all 2020, via Standard — Poco M3, Moto Edge+, Galaxy S20 FE) and **trimmed the
Honey ads out with YouTube's editor** to stop promoting it. His lesson: **"time to get super selective about sponsors
— be even more skeptical about what we put in front of our audience,"** doubling down on long-term channel partners he
trusts (dbrand, Ridge). "Uninstall Honey; if you never installed it, keep it that way."

## Key claims (paraphrase, dated)
- [2024-12-31] **Affiliate-code poaching — Honey steals the referral.** "'Because Honey is the last step before checkout
  for everyone who's installed it, hitting "Apply Honey" removes the creator's affiliate code and replaces it with
  Honey's own — every single time. So Honey collects the affiliate revenue on every sale no matter who actually
  referred you. That alone would be enough for any creator to stop working with them.'"
- [2024-12-31] **The double-speak is the scam — they can't both be true.** "'To users: "use Honey to get the best coupon
  codes." To retailers: "partner with Honey and you choose which codes show up, so you prevent customers from getting
  the best deals" for a 3–5% commission. That double-speak is the core of why it's a scam — you have to be lying to one
  side, and they're lying to the users. As Austin Evans said, they pay creators up front for the ad and steal from
  their back pockets.'"
- [2024-12-31] **Honey is owned by PayPal — this is the business model.** "'The crazy part is it was all in plain sight,
  and Honey is owned by PayPal — this wasn't some shady side thing PayPal didn't know about. This is their revenue,
  this is the thing they do to make money. This is the business model.'"
- [2024-12-31] **I had three Honey ads — I trimmed them out.** "'I had three Honey-sponsored videos, all in 2020 via
  Standard (Poco M3, Moto Edge+, Galaxy S20 FE). I used YouTube's editor to trim the Honey ads out of those videos, so
  I'm not accidentally promoting Honey any more than I have to. If I'd known any of this, I never would have worked with
  them — and neither would the other creators (5,000+ videos, ~8 billion views).'"
- [2024-12-31] **The lesson: be super selective about sponsors.** "'Time to get super selective about sponsors on
  YouTube, especially in 2025 — be even more skeptical about the products and companies we put in front of our
  audience. For me that looks like being extra committed to the long-term channel partners I trust, like dbrand and the
  Ridge collaborations. Uninstall Honey; if you never installed it, keep it that way.'"
