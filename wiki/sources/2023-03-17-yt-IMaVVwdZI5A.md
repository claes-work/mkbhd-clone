## Summary
The episode opens on the **Samsung fake-moon-photo controversy** — the Reddit experiment
proving Samsung pastes AI "fake detail" onto moon shots — which Marques ties directly to
his ongoing **computational-photography / "smartphone cameras just edit reality"** thesis.
(The Pixel-leak of the title is secondary.)

## Key claims (paraphrase, dated 2023-03-17)
- ★ **[Marques, clearly — the moon-photo controversy]** History: **Huawei's P30** (first
  periscope-zoom phone) was accused of faking moon shots years earlier; now **Samsung** is
  caught doing "that exact thing." A **Reddit user** proved it — put a **deliberately
  blurred Photoshop moon on a monitor across the room**, zoomed to 50–100×, shot it, and the
  result came out **dramatically sharper with detail the source image never had** →
  **"fake detail."** Samsung has done this **since ~the S10**, with documentation on its own
  site. *(the case study behind his camera-authenticity views.)*
- ★★ **[Marques, clearly — "what is a photo" doctrine]** His framing: **"smartphone cameras
  just edit reality — they edit all the time."** He points to his own videos — the **iPhone
  camera** deep-dive and **"Smartphone Cameras vs. Reality."** Moon photos are the case
  people **notice** the editing, even though shooting the moon "is not a very common picture
  to take." *(this is the seed of the "what is a photo?" thesis Samsung itself would later
  quote him on — [[2024-02-09-yt-X8gvuyi7oz0]].)*
- ★ **[Marques, clearly — his viral moon short]** "About a month ago I made a short — we had
  a full moon, I zoomed all the way in, posted the clip on Twitter because it was
  impressive, and **it had like 20 million views the next day.**" In it he'd noted "at least
  [Samsung's] not doing the Huawei overlay thing" — before the Reddit proof showed they
  effectively were. *(documents his role in surfacing the controversy.)*
- **[group — musing]** Is the **moon the most-photographed single thing of all time?** It's
  **tidally locked**, so it looks the same from everywhere on Earth every night — so
  everyone's moon photos look "vaguely the same."
